KS3 Science

Particles and gas pressure

Gas particles move randomly and collide with the walls of a container. Heating increases their average speed. In a rigid container, faster collisions with the walls increase the pressure.

Predict. Two identical sealed rigid containers hold the same amount of air. One is warmer. What is different about its particles and its pressure?

Check your prediction

The warmer air has faster-moving particles on average. They hit the walls more frequently and each collision tends to transfer more momentum. The gas pressure is higher. The particles themselves have the same size.

Try it yourself. A sealed syringe is squeezed slowly while the gas stays at room temperature. Explain why the pressure rises even though the average particle speed stays the same.

Compare your explanation

The same particles occupy a smaller volume. They travel a shorter average distance between collisions with the walls, so those collisions are more frequent and the pressure rises.

Cells and microscopes

The nucleus contains genetic information. The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell. A plant cell also has a cell wall. Chloroplasts occur in plant cells that carry out photosynthesis.

Identify. A leaf cell has a rigid outer boundary and several small green structures. Give a name and a function for each.

Compare your answer

Cell wall: supports the cell and helps maintain its shape. Chloroplasts: absorb light for photosynthesis.

Investigate. A pupil sees no green structures in an onion bulb epidermal cell. Explain why this does not rule out a plant cell. Suggest an observation that would support the identification.

Check your reasoning

Onion bulb tissue grows underground and its epidermal cells usually lack chloroplasts. A visible cell wall would support plant-cell identification in this comparison, alongside the source of the tissue.

Adaptation and variation

Individuals of one species can vary. In a particular environment, a feature can improve the chances of surviving and reproducing. If the feature is inherited, it can become more common over generations.

Predict. Light and dark insects of one species live on light bark. Birds find dark insects more easily. Which insects are more likely to survive?

Check your prediction

Light insects are harder to see on light bark, so more may survive. Individual insects do not change colour because they need to hide.

Apply. If the bark becomes darker, explain what may happen to the proportion of dark insects over several generations. State the assumption you need about colour.

Compare your explanation

Dark insects may become better camouflaged, so more survive and reproduce. If colour is inherited, their offspring are more likely to be dark and the proportion of dark insects may increase.

KS3 · Explain a change

Worked question. Explain why the pressure rises when air in a sealed rigid container is warmed.

See a worked outline and paragraph

Topic sentence. Warming air in a sealed rigid container increases its pressure.

Notes. Same gas particles and volume → speed increases → more frequent wall collisions → stronger effect per collision.

Closing link. The changed collisions explain the higher pressure.

Warming air in a sealed rigid container increases its pressure. The container keeps the same volume and no air escapes. Heating increases the average speed of the gas particles. They therefore hit the walls more often. Faster particles also transfer more momentum when they collide with a wall. Together these changes increase the pressure on the walls.

Your turn. Explain why a drop of food colouring spreads faster through warm water than through cold water, when both are left unstirred.

Start with this topic sentence. Food colouring spreads faster through warm water. Plan the rest of the outline.

Practise ordering the notes first

Put these ideas in an order you could explain: the colour spreads sooner · particles move faster · the water is warmer.

Start with the warmer water, then the faster random movement, then how this affects the spread of colour. Explain each link in that sequence.

Compare your explanation

Look for faster random movement at the higher temperature, movement in all directions and a net spread from the area of higher concentration. Check that you explained the difference in speed and that your sentences form a connected explanation.

KS4 · Use evidence and explain it

Worked question. In a model, amylase produces the same amount of product in 80 seconds at 15 °C and in 26 seconds at 35 °C. All other conditions are held constant. Compare and explain these results.

See a worked outline and paragraph

Topic sentence. Amylase worked faster at 35 °C across the two temperatures tested.

Notes. 26 s vs 80 s → same amount of product → faster rate → higher kinetic energy → more frequent successful collisions → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second.

Closing link. The comparison does not establish the optimum.

Amylase worked faster at 35 °C across the two temperatures tested. The same amount of product formed in 26 seconds at 35 °C and in 80 seconds at 15 °C. Producing the same amount in less time gives a greater mean rate. At the higher temperature the enzyme and substrate molecules move faster on average. This increases the frequency of successful collisions and more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second. We would need results from more temperatures to identify the optimum.

Your turn. Potato cylinders gained 7% mass in dilute sugar solution and lost 9% in concentrated sugar solution. The cylinders were the same size and immersed for the same time. Explain the results and why the two readings do not tell you the exact concentration at which mass would stay constant.

Compare with the key ideas

Use both percentages. Explain net movement of water by osmosis through partially permeable cell membranes: into the cells in the dilute solution and out of them in concentrated solution. The results bracket a change of sign under these conditions. Measurements at intermediate concentrations are needed to estimate the concentration giving no net change in mass.

A-level · Develop a paragraph for the essay

Worked essay title. The importance of gradients in living organisms.

See a worked outline and paragraph

Topic sentence. A proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane enables ATP synthesis during aerobic respiration.

Notes. Reduced coenzymes donate electrons → electron transfer releases energy → H⁺ pumped to intermembrane space → H⁺ diffuses through ATP synthase → ATP made → energy transfer for cell processes.

Closing link. The gradient couples energy released by electron transfer to ATP production.

A proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane enables ATP synthesis during aerobic respiration. Reduced coenzymes donate electrons to the electron transport chain. Energy released as electrons pass along the chain drives the pumping of protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space. Protons then move back into the matrix through ATP synthase down their electrochemical gradient. This movement drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP can then transfer energy to processes such as active transport. The proton gradient is therefore the link between electron transfer and the supply of ATP for cellular work.

Your turn. Plan and write a different paragraph for the same title, using sodium–glucose co-transport in the ileum. Then note two other topics you could use in the full essay and what each would add.

Check mechanism, relevance and breadth

Sodium ions are actively pumped from the epithelial cell towards the blood, keeping intracellular sodium concentration low. Sodium moves into the cell from the lumen down its gradient through a co-transporter, carrying glucose with it. Glucose can then move into the blood by facilitated diffusion. Connect the gradient to the benefit of absorbing glucose even where its concentration is lower in the lumen. Other paragraphs could cover maintaining diffusion gradients at exchange surfaces and ion gradients in nerve impulses, each with its own mechanism and significance.

Build the essay. Apply the outline to a second idea from a different topic. Check that each paragraph makes a different contribution to the same essay title.

Biology · Amylase and pH

Iodine is blue-black when starch is present. At pH 5, the last blue-black spot appears at 60 s and the first brown spot at 75 s. At pH 7, the equivalent readings are 30 s and 45 s.

Which mixture digested the starch faster? Why were the samples tested at regular intervals, and why should both mixtures be kept at the same temperature?

Check your reasoning

At pH 7, the iodine test first showed no detectable starch sooner, so digestion was faster under the conditions tested. Sampling at regular intervals makes the endpoint estimates comparable. Temperature affects enzyme activity, so controlling it helps isolate the effect of pH. The actual endpoint falls between samples.

Practise Organisation

Chemistry · Chromatography

On a paper chromatogram, the solvent front travels 8.0 cm. Two spots from one ink sample travel 2.0 cm and 6.0 cm from the baseline.

Calculate each Rf value. Explain why the baseline should be drawn in pencil and start above the solvent.

Compare your answer

Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front: 0.25 and 0.75. Graphite does not dissolve in the usual solvent and form extra spots. The ink starts above the solvent so it moves up with the solvent front instead of dissolving into the solvent reservoir.

Does stirring increase the initial rate of oxygen uptake?

Students used equal masses of germinating seeds, kept at the same temperature. They measured oxygen uptake per gram in stirred and unstirred chambers. Each result below comes from a different batch of seeds. The batches in each group were drawn from different seed packets.

  1. Compare the group means and comment on the individual measurements.
  2. Explain why these results are insufficient to attribute the difference to stirring.
  3. Propose a better allocation of seeds and a control for changes due to the apparatus.
Compare with a reasoned answer

The mean is 3.80 without stirring and 4.30 with stirring. The ranges overlap and there are only three independent batches per treatment. Packet and treatment are confounded, so different germination or respiration between packets could explain some of the difference. Divide seeds from each packet between both conditions, randomly allocating comparable batches. Repeat with several independent packets, and run seed-free chambers in both conditions to detect changes in oxygen readings due to the apparatus or stirring alone.

Continue Paper 3 evidence practice

Do the measurements establish the optimum?

Students measured the initial rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. The mean rates were 1.2, 2.0, 2.3 and 1.1 µmol min⁻¹ respectively. They started timing immediately after adding enzyme solution taken from a refrigerator.

  1. State the strongest conclusion permitted by these results.
  2. Explain how the starting procedure might affect the comparison.
  3. Suggest changes that would help locate the optimum and quantify uncertainty.
Compare with a reasoned answer

40 °C gave the largest measured mean among the tested temperatures. The true optimum could lie between tested points. Adding cold enzyme can lower the temperature of the mixture, and the reaction may begin before the mixture reaches the intended temperature. Equilibrate the separate solutions before mixing, check the temperature at the start and maintain it with a water bath. Use smaller temperature intervals around the peak and independent repeats at each temperature to estimate variation.

Enzyme investigations

Predict, test in the model, then answer the follow-up from memory. The simulation reports modelled rates in arbitrary units.

KS3 · Which enzyme digests starch?

Choose starch. Predict what will happen with stomach protease alone. Remove salivary amylase, add four protease molecules and start. Restart, swap to four amylase molecules and compare the product counts.

Open the KS3 model task

After the model. Could adding more protease make it digest starch? Explain.

Check your explanation

Protease acts on proteins. Its active site is unsuitable for starch, so extra protease does not solve the mismatch. Amylase has an active site that can bind starch.

GCSE · Compare two temperatures

Set standard conditions before each run. Try 20 °C and 37 °C with the same enzyme, pH and starting amount of starch. Predict which has the higher starting rate and compare their modelled peak rates.

Open the GCSE model task

After the model. Explain why activity is lower in a third run at a much higher temperature.

Check your explanation

Warming within this lower range increases the rate of successful collisions. At sufficiently high temperatures the enzyme denatures. Its active site changes shape, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.

A-level · Test for saturation

Start from standard conditions before each trial. Keep enzyme concentration, temperature and pH constant. Try starting substrate concentrations 5, 20 and 40. Compare the peak modelled rates; these capture rates before much substrate has been used up.

Open the A-level model task

After the model. Sketch initial rate against starting substrate concentration. Explain the flattening and predict how doubling enzyme concentration changes the plateau.

Compare with the key ideas

The initial-rate curve rises and approaches a plateau as active sites increasingly become occupied. Extra substrate then has less effect on the rate. More enzyme supplies more active sites and raises the maximum rate. The model’s rate-over-time plot is a different graph: you use a rate from each trial to construct the substrate-concentration plot.
