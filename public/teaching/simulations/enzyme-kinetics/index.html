The science behind the model

Specificity. Each enzyme has a differently shaped active site. Only a substrate with a complementary shape can bind, so amylase cannot digest protein however much of it you add.

Two different temperature effects. Cold isn't denaturing — molecules simply move slowly, so there are fewer collisions, and warming restores the rate. Heat above the optimum denatures the enzyme: the shape is ruined permanently and does not return on cooling.

pH does both. A small shift from the optimum distorts the active site and slows the enzyme, and that part is reversible. An extreme pH breaks the bonds holding the protein's shape and denatures it permanently — which is why stomach acid destroys salivary amylase once food is swallowed, and why pepsin is finished once it reaches the alkaline small intestine.

Arbitrary units. Rate is shown in AU, scaled so that the standard conditions (the default concentrations, 37 °C, optimum pH) give a rate of 1.00 AU. Real rates would be measured as product formed per unit volume per second (mol dm-3 s-1); AU lets you compare conditions without inventing units the model can't justify.

Saturation. With a fixed number of enzymes, adding more substrate stops increasing the rate once every active site is always occupied — that ceiling is the maximum rate.
