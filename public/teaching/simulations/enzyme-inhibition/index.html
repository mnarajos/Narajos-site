The science behind the model

Competitive inhibition. The inhibitor is a similar shape to the substrate and is complementary to part of the active site, so the two compete for it. Only one can occupy the site at a time, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form. Raising the substrate concentration means substrate wins the competition more often, so the inhibition is overcome and the same Vmax is still reached — it just takes more substrate.

Non-competitive inhibition. The inhibitor binds the allosteric site, a site other than the active site. This changes the tertiary structure, so the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate and no enzyme–substrate complex can form. Adding more substrate cannot help, because substrate and inhibitor are not competing for the same site, so Vmax is lowered.

Both are reversible here. Each inhibitor stays bound only for a while and then leaves, free to bind again. Inhibitors are not used up and do not denature the enzyme.

What the chamber shows and what the graph computes. The chamber illustrates the mechanism — which molecule binds where, and what is turned away. The rate readout and the graphs come from the kinetic model above, so they are exact and repeatable rather than depending on the luck of individual collisions. Counting products by eye over a few seconds will be noisy, exactly as a real experiment is.

Arbitrary units. Rate is scaled so 1.00 AU is Vmax for 4 enzymes at 37 °C and optimum pH with no inhibitor — the rate approached when every active site is saturated with substrate.
