Educational research has helped us understand how pupils learn and how teachers can support that learning. However, knowing which teaching strategies are effective is not quite the same as knowing when and how to use them.

Retrieval practice, scaffolding, and feedback can all support learning, but their effectiveness depends on what pupils already know, what they are learning, and what they are finding difficult.

An important distinction is that between learning and performance (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015). Pupils may complete a task successfully during a lesson but struggle to do something similar a week later. Equally, pupils who find an activity difficult may still be learning from it.

I consider four characteristics of knowledge particularly important: how much pupils know, how accurate their knowledge is, how readily they can retrieve it, and how well they can connect different ideas.

Alongside developing knowledge, I believe teachers have an important role in helping pupils become more independent learners. This involves teaching pupils how to evaluate their understanding, recognise gaps in their knowledge, and make better decisions about how they study.

Drawing on educational research and my experiences as a teacher, I have developed a framework for understanding how these aspects of learning and teaching interact. I am particularly interested in identifying what is limiting pupils’ learning and where teachers can make the greatest difference.

The principles presented here inform both my classroom practice and the design of the learning resources on this website. Although many of the same principles apply to independent learning, a website cannot replicate the professional judgement of a classroom teacher.

Thinking about learning and teaching together

The diagram below is a way of organising my thinking, rather than a model that has itself been tested. It brings together several relationships that matter when I plan and adapt teaching.

What pupils already know affects how they make sense of new information. Instruction and practice can develop that knowledge, which then becomes part of the foundation for later learning. Teachers use what pupils say, write, and do as evidence, while recognising that performance is an imperfect guide to learning. Task design and support can also affect what pupils are able to show.

The arrows show relationships worth considering, not a fixed teaching sequence or guaranteed outcomes. Conditions for learning support the whole process. Motivation and self-regulation are distinct influences; neither develops in just one way.

Create the conditions for learning

Pupils need opportunities to think, practise, and contribute in an environment where they know what is expected and feel able to take part. Predictable routines can make lesson time more productive. Trusting relationships can also make it easier for pupils to ask for help or risk an answer. The EEF’s guidance on behaviour emphasises understanding pupils and establishing consistent approaches, while recognising that schools work in different contexts (EEF, 2019; Coe et al., 2020).

I might give pupils time to think before asking for responses, or use a short written answer so that I can see more than a few raised hands. Participation matters, but visible activity is not the same as thinking. A pupil who is quiet may be thinking carefully, while a pupil who answers quickly may not yet have a secure explanation.

On Narajos, clear instructions and predictable activity structures aim to help learners concentrate on the science. They cannot create the relationships, knowledge of individual pupils, or classroom culture that a teacher develops.

Build on secure foundations

New ideas are easier to connect and use when relevant prior knowledge is available. Ausubel’s work drew attention to the importance of what learners already know (Ausubel, 1968). I take this as a reason to consider which earlier ideas a new topic genuinely depends on, and to check those ideas before deciding what needs to be revisited.

For example, before teaching how surface area affects exchange, I might ask pupils to explain diffusion and identify the features of a surface. If answers reveal a gap, a brief explanation and a few carefully chosen questions may be more useful than repeating an entire earlier unit.

I use mastery as a working description of “Sufficiently secure, accurate, accessible, and independently usable knowledge or competence, relative to a specified learning objective”. It does not mean perfect performance or permanent retention, and it is not a rigid threshold that must always be crossed before new learning can begin.

Sometimes a new topic provides a useful reason to revisit an earlier one. On Narajos, catch-up routes and sequenced explanations offer ways to revisit selected knowledge, but they do not establish every prerequisite a pupil may need.

Make complexity manageable

Working memory is limited: we can only attend to and process a limited amount of new information at once. Cognitive load theory considers how the demands of a task and the way it is presented interact with what a learner already knows (Sweller, van Merriënboer, and Paas, 2019).

Some material has high element interactivity: several ideas must be understood in relation to one another. Its difficulty depends partly on the learner’s existing knowledge. High interactivity is not a flaw to remove. Temporarily teaching components separately can help some learners, but understanding how those components interact is often the ultimate goal (Pollock, Chandler, and Sweller, 2002).

I try to remove demands that do not serve the learning goal. A clear diagram placed beside its explanation, for example, may be more useful than asking pupils to search between several pages. I might introduce a complex mechanism in parts before asking pupils to explain how the parts work together.

Managing complexity is not the same as avoiding it. Some Narajos activities use short explanations, diagrams, and sequenced questions to make an initial encounter more manageable before asking learners to apply the ideas.

Guide pupils towards independence

Explicit instruction makes important thinking visible. I can model how to approach a question, explain a decision, and then guide pupils through a similar problem before expecting them to work more independently (Rosenshine, 2012).

Scaffolding is temporary support that helps a learner complete a task they could not yet manage in the same way alone. It might be a prompt, a partially completed example, or a carefully chosen question (Wood, Bruner, and Ross, 1976). I can adjust the challenge of a task and the support I provide separately: a demanding problem can be well supported, and a familiar task can be completed independently.

Support should be reduced when evidence suggests that it is no longer needed for the learning goal, rather than according to a fixed timetable. Support that helps one learner may become unnecessary or distracting as their knowledge grows (Kalyuga et al., 2003). This does not mean withdrawing an accessibility adjustment that a pupil continues to need.

On Narajos, worked examples, hints, and guided questions are available in some activities. Their purpose is to help learners make progress towards understanding, not to make every task easy.

Make learning durable and connected

Retrieval practice means bringing information to mind from memory. A short question answered without looking at notes is retrieval practice; copying a completed answer is practice of a different kind. Research suggests that retrieval can support later retention, although the useful form and timing depend on the material and the learner (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006; Dunlosky et al., 2013).

I use retrieval alongside explanation, feedback, and other forms of practice. Revisiting ideas over time can reveal what has been retained. Asking pupils to compare ideas, explain a connection, or apply knowledge in a different context can also reveal whether they can use it beyond the original example.

Difficulty is not automatically productive. If pupils cannot retrieve enough to make a useful attempt, a prompt or a return to the explanation may be needed. Narajos includes recall activities, as well as questions that invite explanation and application. The GCSE Combined Science and A-level Biology flashcards also offer spaced review, using previous responses on the device to identify cards due for review.

Check understanding and respond

Assessment becomes formative when evidence of learning is used to make a useful decision about what happens next. A question may reveal that several pupils have chosen the correct answer, but it may not reveal whether they understand the reasoning. I therefore look for patterns across answers and consider alternative explanations before deciding whether to move on, revisit, or change the task (Black and Wiliam, 1998).

Making these decisions calls for secure subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge: knowledge of how to explain and teach particular ideas, including the difficulties pupils may encounter (Shulman, 1986).

Feedback is related to assessment, but it is not the same thing. Providing information is only a beginning: pupils need to interpret it, decide what it means, and use it. For feedback to help, they need to understand the goal, recognise something about their current work, and have an opportunity to act (Sadler, 1989; Butler and Winne, 1995). The aim is to improve subsequent learning, not merely to correct a completed piece of work. I also need to check whether my response has helped; repeating the same explanation is not always enough.

In my article “Feedback is not a gift” (2024), I explored pupils’ engagement with feedback in a particular classroom context. It informs my thinking about pupils’ active role in feedback, but it does not establish that one approach will work everywhere. The EEF similarly emphasises the principles and use of feedback, rather than prescribing a single delivery method (EEF, 2021).

Narajos can offer feedback on some responses and prompts for learners to compare or improve their work. That feedback is necessarily limited by the activity’s design. It cannot interpret every answer or replace a teacher’s knowledge of a pupil.

Build motivation through meaningful success

Success can help pupils believe that they are capable of making progress. Self-efficacy is a person’s belief about their ability to carry out a particular task or meet a particular challenge; it is more specific than general confidence. Experience of success is one possible influence on that belief, alongside factors such as observing others and receiving feedback (Bandura, 1977).

I want pupils to succeed at worthwhile work, not simply accumulate easy correct answers. That may mean providing enough support for a pupil to attempt a demanding explanation, recognising a sound part of an answer, and making the next step clear. It also means checking that the challenge remains appropriate as knowledge develops.

Success will not automatically produce motivation, and a pupil’s effort or participation should not be explained by motivation alone. Task design, prior knowledge, relationships, accessibility, and circumstances all matter. On Narajos, sequenced questions and feedback are intended to give learners opportunities to recognise progress, without assuming that the same experience will motivate everyone.

Develop self-regulating learners

Metacognition involves awareness of and thinking about one’s own learning, including knowledge of a task and possible strategies. Self-regulated learning is broader: it includes using that awareness to plan, monitor, and adjust learning, as well as managing motivation, effort, and the learning environment (Zimmerman, 2002; EEF, 2025).

I might model how I decide what a question is asking, check whether an explanation answers it, and choose what to revise next. Pupils can then practise making those decisions with guidance. These strategies are most useful when connected to the knowledge and demands of a particular subject, rather than taught only as a generic checklist. Independent practice alone does not automatically develop self-regulation.

Learners can misjudge what they know, especially when material feels familiar (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Feedback and opportunities to compare a response with a worked explanation can help them monitor understanding, but only if they can interpret and use that information (Butler and Winne, 1995). Some Narajos activities offer these opportunities; learners may still need a teacher’s help to decide what to do next.

What is limiting learning?

When pupils find something difficult, I find it useful to ask what might be limiting their progress before choosing a response. The same observation can have several explanations, and more than one barrier may be present.

The examples below are starting points for professional enquiry. They are not diagnoses or prescriptions. Use them alongside knowledge of the pupils, the subject, and the wider classroom context.

Consider the cost as well as the possible benefit. Every activity uses time and effort that could be spent elsewhere. A short check, a well-chosen explanation, or time for pupils to act on feedback may sometimes be more valuable than a more elaborate intervention.

Pupils appeared to understand the material during the lesson but cannot remember it a week later

Possible explanations: Pupils may have relied on examples or prompts; their initial understanding may have been incomplete; or they may have had little opportunity to revisit the material.

Checks: Compare an independent question with a prompted version. Ask a similar question after a further interval and inspect which parts can still be explained.

Possible responses: Reteach an insecure idea before asking pupils to retrieve it again. Revisit the knowledge at intervals, with feedback, and check whether recall improves.

Pupils know individual facts but struggle to connect or apply them

Possible explanations: The facts may be remembered without relationships between them; the new context may be unfamiliar; or pupils may not recognise which knowledge is relevant.

Checks: Ask pupils to explain a relationship in a familiar example. Compare their response to a worked unfamiliar example with their response to a new one.

Possible responses: Model how to select and connect relevant ideas. Practise applying the same relationship in a small range of contexts, with support and feedback as needed.

Pupils succeed with scaffolding but struggle independently

Possible explanations: Pupils may depend on a prompt to select the next step; the underlying knowledge may be insecure; or the independent task may introduce additional demands.

Checks: Compare the supported and independent tasks for differences in content and difficulty. Remove one instructional prompt at a time and ask pupils to explain their choices.

Possible responses: Model the decision that a prompt was helping pupils make, then practise it with gradually reduced support. Revisit missing knowledge or reduce an unrelated demand before trying again.

Accessibility adjustments should remain available where needed. They are not necessarily temporary instructional scaffolds.

Pupils repeatedly demonstrate the same misconception

Possible explanations: A prior idea may still seem plausible; pupils may be applying a rule too broadly; or the question or feedback may not reveal what they are thinking.

Checks: Ask pupils to explain their reasoning in more than one context. Compare their explanation with a contrasting example or a carefully chosen misconception question.

Possible responses: Make the distinction between the ideas explicit using an example and a counterexample. Give pupils another opportunity to explain and apply the corrected idea, then recheck later.

Pupils give up too quickly

Possible explanations: The task may exceed available knowledge; pupils may be unsure how to begin; or previous experiences, fatigue, accessibility needs, or confidence may affect their response.

Checks: Ask pupils which part feels difficult and observe an initial attempt. Offer a small starting prompt and see whether it helps them continue.

Possible responses: Model an entry point and provide a manageable first step. Adjust the task or support, attend to access needs, and recognise specific progress without assuming that the problem is simply a lack of motivation.

High-attaining pupils are not progressing towards the highest grades

Possible explanations: Pupils may have gaps in precise knowledge; they may struggle to select and connect ideas in unfamiliar questions; or they may have limited practice with the demands of extended responses.

Checks: Examine a range of work for patterns against the relevant criteria. Compare independent responses to familiar and unfamiliar tasks, and ask pupils to explain their choices.

Possible responses: Address a specific gap with targeted explanation and practice. Model the reasoning required in a demanding response, then provide opportunities for independent application and feedback.

Only a small proportion of pupils participate in questioning

Possible explanations: Pupils may need more thinking time; the question or language may be unclear; or the classroom routine may make answering feel risky.

Checks: Collect a brief response from everyone using an accessible method. Compare the pattern after individual thinking time or a change in how the question is presented.

Possible responses: Give pupils time to prepare an answer and provide more than one way to respond. Clarify the question or model a first step, while remembering that visible participation is not a complete measure of thought.

Pupils receive feedback but do not make effective use of it

Possible explanations: Pupils may not understand the feedback; they may not know what action to take; or they may lack the time or knowledge needed to act.

Checks: Ask pupils to explain the feedback and identify their next step. Review their subsequent work to see what they changed and whether it helped.

Possible responses: Model how to use a piece of feedback and give pupils time to act on it. Clarify the intended improvement or reteach the necessary knowledge, then review the result.
