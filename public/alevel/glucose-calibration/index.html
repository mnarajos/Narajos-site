Glucose calibration curve

Make a dilution series of glucose, use Benedict's and a colorimeter to produce a calibration curve, and find the glucose concentration of "urine" samples. Start with the core investigation, or open any method in Explore.

Benedict’s detects reducing sugars, not glucose alone. Here the samples are simulated and contain glucose as the only reducing sugar.

Two Benedict's methods

Qualitative

What is measured
Light blocked by the red-brown precipitate (copper(I) oxide) suspended in the liquid.
Preparing the cuvette
Shake the tube, fill the cuvette and read at once: the precipitate settles.
As glucose rises
Absorbance increases: more precipitate. It levels off as the Cu²⁺ runs short.
Handbook filter
600 nm (orange).
Limitations
Readings fall if the precipitate settles; a curved calibration, less precise at high glucose.

Quantitative

What is measured
Light absorbed by the blue Cu²⁺ ions left in solution.
Preparing the cuvette
Let the white precipitate settle (or centrifuge), then take only the clear liquid.
As glucose rises
Absorbance decreases: more Cu²⁺ is used up, so less blue is left.
Handbook filter
680 nm (red).
Limitations
Any precipitate in the cuvette scatters light and raises the reading; small volumes are hard to separate cleanly.

Blanks depend on the procedure. The AQA example zeroes with a heated zero-glucose tube, which cancels the Benedict's own absorbance. Distilled water is a different reference: it does not cancel Benedict's own absorbance, so that is included in every reading, but a calibration still works if every standard and sample is read against the same blank. The AQA handbook says the quantitative method gives more reliable results.

Reaction close-up (schematic, not to scale)

Cu²⁺ ion (blue) Glucose (reducing sugar) Glucose that has reacted Sucrose (non-reducing) Copper(I): joins into precipitate

Schematic. The numbers of particles are arbitrary: they do not show the real ratio of glucose to Cu²⁺, and each dot stands for a very large number. What it does show: a Cu²⁺ ion is reduced only when glucose touches it, and only when hot; copper(I) joins into precipitate; when the Cu²⁺ is used up, extra glucose changes nothing.

Light close-up: inside the colorimeter

White light contains many wavelengths; the filter lets one colour through. Blue Cu²⁺ ions absorb red light strongly and orange light less, so the beam dims as it crosses the cuvette. Precipitate and fingerprints scatter light out of the beam. Less light at the detector than with the blank means a higher absorbance.

Results

These results are simulated: the model's assumptions
  • Published and simulated data. The values labelled "Published AQA readings" come from the AQA practical handbook. Its text reports 4.7 mmol dm⁻³ for a 0.89 reading, but its own table brackets 0.89 between the 2 and 4 mmol dm⁻³ standards. The worked interpolation uses the table. All lab readings here are simulated; Apply graphs are illustrative data made up for the questions.
  • The method follows the AQA practical handbook: standards of 0 to 10 mmol dm⁻³ made from a 10 mmol dm⁻³ standard, 2 cm³ of each sample and 2 cm³ of Benedict's, 4 minutes in a 90 °C water bath, cooled before reading. Katniss, Arya and Carrie's samples contain 0, 5 and 8 mmol dm⁻³ glucose.
  • Qualitative Benedict's is read shaken, at 600 nm, against the heated 0 tube. Its readings are fitted to the handbook's sample results (0.76 at 2, 1.39 at 10 mmol dm⁻³). Quantitative Benedict's is read at 680 nm against water; the blue Cu²⁺ left absorbs about 0.80 with no glucose.
  • The Cu²⁺ in 2 cm³ of either reagent is assumed to be used up by about 11 mmol dm⁻³ of glucose (illustrative), so higher concentrations give almost the same reading.
  • Percentage transmission is relative to the selected blank (100 × 10−A); negative absorbance relative to a darker blank can therefore give more than 100% relative transmission.
  • Each reading varies randomly by up to about ±0.01. Fingerprints add 0.04 to 0.12 to some cuvettes. Unshaken precipitate settles over time; precipitate poured into a quantitative cuvette adds to the absorbance.
  • A fitted line is only a model: inspect the points, replicates and sensitivity before trusting it. Estimates are withheld for simulated handling errors and outside the observed range. A narrower standard range can improve local definition but does not guarantee accuracy or remove systematic errors. The curve of best fit is calculated, and drawn only between the lowest and highest standards. The reaction close-up is schematic: its particle numbers are arbitrary. The light close-up's percentages come from the same absorbance model as the lab.
