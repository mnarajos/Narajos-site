Co-transport: same direction, different gradients

A 45-minute homework · Year 12 · self-marked

Write your numbered answers and diagrams in your exercise book. Attempt each group before opening Check and improve. Use the screen to mark and improve your own work; the site does not assess your book. The times include checking and improving.

Key idea: Two substances can move in the same direction across a membrane while moving in opposite directions relative to their own concentration gradients.

For each substance: 1. Identify it. 2. Compare its concentration on the two sides. 3. Identify its direction of movement. 4. Decide whether it moves down (high to low) or against (low to high) its concentration gradient. Compare sodium with sodium, not with glucose.

Arrows show where substances travel: solid blue arrows show ions (Na⁺, K⁺ or H⁺), and dashed brown arrows show the sugar. Labels identify every substance. “Down” and “against” describe concentration differences, not the physical direction of the arrow. Ion movement also depends on electrical gradients; in this homework use the supplied concentrations as a simplified model.

A · Retrieve the principle

About 5 minutes, including checking

In this illustrative ileum example, sodium ions and glucose both enter an epithelial cell through a co-transporter. Concentrations are arbitrary units.

  1. Copy a table with rows for Na⁺ and glucose and columns: concentration at start, concentration at destination, movement (high to low or low to high), and down or against its gradient. Complete it.
  2. How can sodium and glucose both enter the cell but move differently relative to their gradients?
Check and improve · questions 1–2

Na⁺: 100 → 10, high to low, down its gradient. Glucose: 2 → 8, low to high, against its gradient. Both travel into the cell, but each has its own concentration difference. Sodium entry down its gradient drives glucose entry against its gradient through the same co-transporter.

Correction: “Into the cell” does not mean against a gradient, and travelling together does not mean both move down. Improve your table or explanation by naming both sides for each substance.

B · Recovering glucose in the kidney

About 15 minutes, including checking

Kidney tubules contain fluid filtered from the blood. Useful glucose must be recovered from this fluid. Glucose crosses a tubule epithelial cell before returning towards the blood. First focus on the tubule-facing membrane. Values are illustrative concentrations in arbitrary units.

  1. Copy a simplified version of the tubule-facing membrane, with its co-transporter and the two compartments. Add labelled arrows for sodium and glucose.
  2. Draw a table with a row for each substance and columns for starting compartment, destination, high to low or low to high, and down or against its concentration gradient. Complete it.
  3. Sodium ions and glucose both enter the epithelial cell. Explain why only glucose moves against its concentration gradient in this example.
Check and improve · questions 3–5

Both labelled arrows point from tubule fluid through the co-transporter into the epithelial cell. Na⁺: tubule fluid → cell, 100 → 10, high to low, down its gradient. Glucose: tubule fluid → cell, 2 → 8, low to high, against its gradient.

Each substance must be compared with itself across the membrane: sodium concentration is higher outside than inside, whereas glucose concentration is lower outside than inside. The direction of travel alone does not decide the gradient. Add the relevant values to an incomplete explanation in your book.

The rest of the pathway

At the opposite membrane, a sodium–potassium pump uses ATP hydrolysis to move sodium out of the cell and potassium into it. A separate glucose transporter allows glucose to leave the cell by facilitated diffusion. In this example, cell glucose concentration is 8 and tissue fluid glucose concentration is 2 arbitrary units. Tissue fluid lies beside a blood capillary; recovered glucose moves towards the blood.

  1. Correct this statement: “The co-transporter hydrolyses ATP to pump sodium ions and glucose into the cell.”
  2. Explain how the sodium–potassium pump enables continued glucose uptake. Include what happens to glucose after it enters the cell.
Check and improve · questions 6–7

The pump, not the co-transporter, hydrolyses ATP. It moves Na⁺ out of the cell, maintaining a low intracellular Na⁺ concentration. Na⁺ then enters from the tubule fluid down its gradient through the co-transporter, driving coupled glucose uptake against glucose’s gradient. Subsequently glucose leaves through a separate transporter by facilitated diffusion, down its gradient towards the tissue fluid and blood.

“Energy released by ATP hydrolysis” is acceptable. Glucose does not always move against its gradient: its direction relative to the gradient depends on the membrane, transporter and concentrations. Improve your answer by linking the pump to the sodium gradient and then to glucose uptake.

C · Disrupt the mechanism

About 5 minutes, including checking

An inhibitor specifically blocks the sodium–potassium pump. The co-transporter itself is not directly blocked.

  1. Predict what happens to the sodium concentration inside the cell over time.
  2. Explain why glucose uptake by co-transport decreases over time. Distinguish the immediate effect from the later changes.
Check and improve · questions 8–9

Immediately, the pump stops removing sodium, but the existing sodium gradient does not disappear instantly. Sodium can still enter down that gradient. Over time sodium accumulates inside, reducing the concentration difference and the driving force for sodium entry. Less sodium entry drives less glucose uptake by co-transport. The inhibitor does not imply that all glucose transport immediately stops.

Improve your answer by adding the words “over time” and explaining why the gradient changes.

D · A plant uses a different ion

About 15 minutes, including checking

In this example, a plant companion cell loads sucrose using hydrogen ion–sucrose co-transport. An ATP-powered pump moves H⁺ out of the cell, maintaining a higher H⁺ concentration outside. H⁺ returns through a co-transporter; its movement drives sucrose uptake against the sucrose concentration gradient. No detailed phloem knowledge is needed.

The diagram gives illustrative concentrations in arbitrary units. The co-transporter arrows are missing for you to add in your book.

  1. Which substance moves down its concentration gradient through the co-transporter, and which moves against its gradient?
  2. Copy a simplified diagram of the membrane and its two proteins. Add labelled arrows for H⁺ and sucrose through the co-transporter and for H⁺ at the pump.
  3. Explain why sucrose uptake depends indirectly on ATP.
Optional hint · questions 10–12

Compare each substance across the membrane separately. Which protein hydrolyses ATP and what concentration difference does it maintain?

Check and improve · questions 10–12

H⁺ enters down its gradient (100 → 10); sucrose enters against its gradient (2 → 8). Both arrows through the co-transporter point from outside into the cell. The pump arrow for H⁺ points out of the cell. The pump uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to maintain the H⁺ gradient. H⁺ returns down that gradient through the co-transporter, driving sucrose uptake against its gradient. Thus ATP is used indirectly for sucrose uptake, not hydrolysed by the co-transporter.

Improve one arrow or add the missing link between the H⁺ gradient and sucrose uptake.

E · Bring the principle together

About 5 minutes, including checking and improving

  1. Explain how kidney epithelial cells and the plant cell can accumulate a substance against its concentration gradient. Describe what the mechanisms have in common and identify the substances involved.
Check and improve · question 14

Give yourself one point for each marking point present (4 points):

  • ATP-powered pumping establishes or maintains an ion gradient.
  • The ion returns through a co-transporter down its gradient.
  • This drives uptake of another substance against its gradient.
  • The kidney example uses Na⁺ and glucose; the plant example uses H⁺ and sucrose.

Equivalent correct wording is acceptable. Model: ATP-powered pumps maintain ion gradients. Na⁺ in the kidney and H⁺ in the plant return down their gradients through co-transporters, driving glucose and sucrose respectively into cells against their own gradients.
