Co-transport: same direction, different gradients

Independent Practice · approximately 20–25 minutes

Write short, numbered answers in your exercise book. Attempt each group before opening Check and improve, then use the screen to mark and improve your own work. The time includes checking.

Key idea: Two substances can move in the same direction across a membrane while moving in opposite directions relative to their own concentration gradients.

Compare each substance with itself on the two sides. “Down” and “against” describe concentration differences, not the physical direction of an arrow. The values in the figures are illustrative concentrations in arbitrary units. Ion movement also depends on electrical gradients; use the supplied concentrations as a simplified model here.

  2. Kidney

1 · Ileum: read a worked diagram

About 5–6 minutes, including checking

Na⁺ and glucose enter an ileum epithelial cell through the same carrier.

Worked comparison: Na⁺ enters the cell from 100 to 10, so it moves down its concentration gradient.

  1. Does glucose move down or against its own concentration gradient? Use the values to explain.
  2. What does Na⁺ moving down its gradient allow glucose to do?
Check and improve · questions 1–2

Glucose enters from 2 to 8, so it moves against its own concentration gradient. Na⁺ moving down its gradient through the same carrier provides the energy for glucose to move against its gradient. Glucose can therefore still enter when its concentration is already higher inside the cell.

In your book, add the glucose values or the energy link if either is missing.

2 · Kidney: apply the idea and explain ATP’s role

About 7–9 minutes, including checking

Fluid filtered from the blood flows through tiny tubes in the kidney. Cells lining the tubes help return glucose to the blood before the remaining fluid becomes urine.

  1. Glucose is already more concentrated inside the lining cell than in the fluid in the tube. Explain how more glucose can still enter the cell.
Check and improve · question 3, then continue to question 4

Na⁺ enters the lining cell down its concentration gradient (80 to 20). Both Na⁺ and glucose move through the same carrier; Na⁺ entry drives glucose into the cell against its own gradient (3 to 9). Glucose then leaves the lining cell through a separate carrier and returns towards the blood.

Improve your answer by linking sodium entry to glucose uptake through the same carrier.

On the opposite membrane of the lining cell, a sodium–potassium pump moves Na⁺ out and K⁺ in. The pump uses ATP.

  1. Which protein uses ATP directly? Explain how its action allows glucose uptake to continue.
Check and improve · question 4

The sodium–potassium pump hydrolyses ATP directly; the carrier that brings Na⁺ and glucose into the cell does not. The pump removes Na⁺ from the cell, maintaining a low Na⁺ concentration inside. Na⁺ can continue to enter down its gradient through the shared carrier, driving glucose uptake against its gradient.

Improve your answer by adding any missing link between ATP, the sodium gradient and glucose uptake.

3 · Sucrose uptake in a leaf cell

About 8–10 minutes, including checking

  1. Name the transport mechanism shown at carrier X. Use the diagram to justify your answer.
  2. Explain where the energy for sucrose uptake comes from.
  3. The H⁺ concentration outside the cell falls from 60 to 15. Other conditions stay the same. Predict the effect on sucrose uptake and explain your answer.
Check and improve · questions 5–7

5. This is co-transport: H⁺ and sucrose move together through the same carrier. H⁺ moves from 60 to 5, down its gradient; sucrose moves from 3 to 12, against its gradient.

6. H⁺ moving down its gradient provides the energy that drives sucrose uptake against its gradient.

7. Sucrose uptake decreases. When external H⁺ falls to 15 while other conditions stay the same, the inward H⁺ concentration gradient is smaller, providing less driving force for co-transport.

In your book, improve one answer by adding a missing link in your explanation.
