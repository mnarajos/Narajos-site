About

Science resources designed to support learning through clear explanations, structured practice and feedback.

Educational philosophy

I believe good teaching begins with clear explanations, carefully structured practice, and an understanding of how students learn. My approach is informed by educational research, with an emphasis on building secure knowledge, addressing misconceptions, and gradually developing students’ confidence and independence.

This philosophy informs the design of the resources on this website, which are intended to complement classroom teaching rather than replace it. The activities draw on principles of explicit instruction, cognitive load theory and formative assessment, incorporating worked examples, carefully sequenced questions, retrieval practice and feedback.

An important aim is to make the resources accessible to novice learners while providing opportunities for more knowledgeable learners to apply, connect and extend their understanding. Scaffolding is provided where needed, with increasing opportunities for independent practice and more demanding applications.

About me

I’m a science educator with a particular interest in the relationship between educational research and classroom practice.

I completed an MSc in Learning and Teaching at the University of Oxford. My dissertation investigated pupils’ engagement with assessment and feedback, particularly the use of whole-class feedback to support metacognition and self-regulated learning.

Work arising from this research was subsequently published in the Chartered College of Teaching’s Impact journal and as a chapter in a book on metacognition:

  • Feedback is not a gift — Impact, Chartered College of Teaching (2024).
  • Solving the Problem of Feedback — Chapter 7 in Teaching Hacks: Fixing Everyday Classroom Issues with Metacognition, Nathan Burns (SAGE, 2024).

Influences

The design of these resources draws on educational research and the work of teachers who have helped translate that research into classroom practice.

Rosenshine’s principles of instruction, research on cognitive load and formative assessment, and work on retrieval practice have informed the use of modelling, scaffolding, structured questioning and feedback throughout the learning activities.

One particular influence is the work of Adam Boxer and Rosalind Walker on Shed Loads of Practice (SLOP). Their emphasis on providing substantial amounts of carefully sequenced practice has informed the question-based activities, which aim to help learners consolidate knowledge, develop fluency and progress towards increasingly demanding applications.

These influences are reflected in the design of the resources, rather than being intended as a substitute for the professional judgement and responsiveness of classroom teachers.
