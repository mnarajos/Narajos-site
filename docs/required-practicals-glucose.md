# RP11 glucose calibration simulation

The [glucose calibration simulation](https://narajos.com/alevel/glucose-calibration/) complements the existing [RP11 practical activity](https://narajos.com/alevel/#practical-11) and [catch-up lesson](https://narajos.com/alevel/#catchup-a6-glucal). The supplied v3 prototype is the source for the Core, Explore methods and Apply sequences; no earlier prototype was available in this task to verify its version history independently. Core has eight tasks; each of the nine Explore cards is optional and independent, and Apply can be completed without a lab run. The activity and its quantitative Benedict’s data remain available.

## Scientific basis and corrections

- [AQA Biology 7402 practical assessment](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/biology/a-level/biology-7402/specification/practical-assessment) specifies a glucose dilution series and colorimetric calibration of an unknown artificial urine sample, while allowing teachers to vary the approach. The [AQA practical handbook](https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/biology/AQA-7401-7402-PHBK.PDF), RP11 student sheet and teacher notes, and [apparatus guide](https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/biology/AQA-7401-7402-P11.PDF) describe 2 cm³ samples, equal reagent volume, four minutes at 90 °C, cooling, a heated zero standard as blank, and 600 nm qualitative and 680 nm quantitative examples. The handbook notes that quantitative Benedict’s forms white precipitate that should be excluded from the cuvette. The existing Narajos quantitative activity instead uses water as its optical reference; both references are explained and the same blank must be used for standards and samples.
- The qualitative model reads a consistently mixed coloured suspension. The quantitative model reads remaining blue copper(II) in the clear liquid, so its trend is opposite. The simulation assumes glucose is the only reducing sugar in its artificial samples. Benedict’s responds to other reducing sugars; one urine result is not a diagnosis.
- **Published-example discrepancy:** the handbook table lists absorbance 0.76 at 2 mmol dm⁻³ and 1.00 at 4 mmol dm⁻³, with Dick’s reading 0.89; its text also reports 4.70 mmol dm⁻³ for Dick. A monotonic calibration through those tabulated standards places 0.89 between 2 and 4, not 4.70. The revised worked example uses those published readings and gives about 3.1 by explicitly stated straight-line interpolation between them. A smooth curve can give a different estimate. Other lab readings and all Apply datasets are simulated or invented, as labelled.
- The prototype’s dilution calculation tracks glucose amount and actual liquid volume. A proportional pipetting error affects one tube; a spill during serial transfer changes that tube’s volume and concentration, and propagates to dependent standards. The optional custom range requires six increasing values from 0 to 10, beginning at 0, in 0.05 mmol dm⁻³ increments; with the model's 2 cm³ final sample this corresponds to 0.01 cm³ increments in stock volume. A real laboratory would choose apparatus and concentrations appropriate to its precision.
- A fitted calibration is shown only over the standard concentrations. Concentration estimates are withheld outside the observed reading range, for a visibly poor fit, and when the simulation knows that pipetting, heating or handling was wrong. The illustrative fit guard compares the largest residual with the greater of 0.045 absorbance units (or 4 percentage points) and 8% of the observed span; it is a teaching safeguard, not a statistical validation of a calibration. The comparison of an intentionally poor straight line is retained as a labelled teaching example. Close spacing can help define a local calibration but does not automatically improve accuracy or remove systematic error. Flat regions amplify reading uncertainty; repeatability, fit, reagent saturation and systematic errors are distinct issues.
- Percentage transmission is relative to the selected blank: `100 × 10^(−A)`. Negative absorbance relative to a more absorbing blank can give a relative transmission greater than 100%. The transmission curve is the transformed absorbance fit, so corresponding readings agree. The reaction-particle view is schematic: particle counts and collisions do not represent real stoichiometric proportions or reaction kinetics.

## Model and assessment limits

The model assumes an illustrative reagent capacity equivalent to 2 cm³ of an 11 mmol dm⁻³ glucose solution. The reacted fraction uses `1 − exp(−t/1.25)` at 90 °C, capped by the modelled glucose amount and reagent capacity. The qualitative optical response is an empirical saturating function chosen to resemble the handbook example, not a prediction of actual particle scattering or proof that a real curve flattens solely because reagent is exhausted. Quantitative absorbance models blue copper(II) remaining; filter sensitivity, precipitate scattering, fingerprint effects and random variation are illustrative. A readout is rounded to two decimal places. These assumptions should not be treated as measured reagent chemistry, a clinical assay or a replacement for practical work.

Local storage keeps settings, drafts, progress and completed run snapshots on this browser and device, separately from course progress. Run-bound numerical and control tasks retain their evidence; an explicitly selected newer run clears the old response and keeps previous support or mistakes in its achievement status. Bound evidence is retained when unneeded runs are trimmed. An interrupted animation restarts with its selected settings and no invented result. Prediction corrections must be checked; revealed construction lines count as support. Written work is explicitly self-assessed and can be revised. No automatic keyword marking is used.
