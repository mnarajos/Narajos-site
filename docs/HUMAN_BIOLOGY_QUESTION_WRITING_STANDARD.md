# Human Biology — question-writing and diagram standard

**Status:** working authoring standard · 21 November 2026 · Cambridge OCR Advanced National Human Biology (H049/H149).

Based on the Oracle pedagogy project, the AQA A-level Biology question-development work, OCR specification v8 (October 2026), and the limitations of a free, browser-only revision application. This is a standard for **independent learning**, not official OCR assessment material.

## 1. Questions must teach as well as assess

The default path is **achievable recall → precise core terminology → explaining a mechanism → discriminating between plausible explanations → application using a diagram or data → wider transfer**. Allow early success, especially when students have not been taught every biological term. A correct answer after a hint is different from an independently retrieved answer; only the latter supports a claim about independent recall.

Within a concept lesson, keep linked questions together. In Quick Mix, begin with two genuinely easy questions and two core questions before application; retain topic and format variety within those stages. Offer a manageable task after early difficulty, not an abrupt challenge. A mistake needs a specific explanation and, when an appropriate question exists, another independent check **of the same idea**.

## 2. Student-facing language

- **Default to a single short question** rather than a long paragraph with multiple demands. Aim for around 8–20 words for recall and 15–35 for typical applications; use longer passages only when interpreting that passage *is itself* the assessed skill.
- Use **everyday wording at first**, then introduce OCR's scientific term in the answer or a subsequent question. Do not dilute the actual biology or invent a technically inaccurate shortcut.
- Avoid nested clauses, implied background knowledge, hidden negatives and double-barrelled commands. Use "Which …?", "What …?", "Select TWO", "Put in order", "Explain why", "Calculate" and "Use the graph".
- Put numbers in a table or graph when reading them there is important. Use **consistent real units**, sensible precision and worked steps on feedback.
- Do not load the same question with unfamiliar vocabulary *and* unfamiliar methods *and* unfamiliar clinical data. For an unfamiliar context, supply the new facts needed.
- Avoid excessive feedback. In most questions: the correct answer, one or two sentences explaining why, and the misconception it distinguishes.

## 3. Options, alternatives and distractors

- Ordinary multiple-choice questions and each stage of a two-step choice-and-justify question use **four plausible answers**, not three. Shuffle their order, except numeric/ordinal sequences or labelled options that must be ordered.
- Each wrong option must represent a plausible **alternative conception**, often a genuine student or exam mistake (e.g., FSH versus LH, sign versus symptom, scanning versus transmission electron microscopy, treatment versus prevention, assisted reproduction versus tubal repair).
- Avoid nonsense distractors, identical alternatives, two defensible answers, clues from answer length, awkwardly distinct grammatical structures, or making the correct answer consistently the most detailed.
- Preserve correct science and exact terminology in the *answer*. Correct a flawed option by rewriting a distractor, not by weakening a technically necessary definition.
- Question banks should tag the **concept and misconception**. A good "choose and justify" question asks for the right conclusion *and* the biological mechanism, not simply two unrelated guesses.
- For two-step questions, four options must be genuinely distinguishable at both stages. The reasons should test why the chosen explanation is biologically justified, not whether its wording is shorter.

## 4. Useful diversity, not feature proliferation

Use the answer format that reveals the targeted understanding:

| Student's task | Format that often works |
|---|---|
| Identify a structure or recognise a concept | Four-option choice or clickable diagram labels |
| Distinguish two related biological categories | Sort or Venn-type classification |
| Link terms to roles | Matching (ideally 3–5 pairs) |
| Understand the order of a mechanism | Sequencing or build-the-chain |
| Apply a rule to numerical data | Calculation with units and worked feedback |
| Interpret a graph or laboratory readout | A graph/image with a focused question |
| Diagnose a misconception | Spot-the-error or choose-and-justify |
| Explain a mechanism in scientific language | Short response with an explicit self-assessment model |
| Evaluate an unfamiliar claim | Brief provided evidence plus a constrained conclusion |

Keep a short lesson to around 6–12 purposeful questions. Never require long typed essays inside the app; for longer responses, use a short written answer, model points and pupil self-check without AI marking or invented OCR marks.

## 5. Hints that genuinely help

1. **Prompt:** a strategy that does not give scientific content away.
2. **Clue:** one authored, question-specific cue pointing to the key discriminating principle, not the complete answer.
3. **Narrow:** remove **one** wrong option from a four-choice question, give a first step of a long sequence or another small portion of support. Never reveal every missing word, the correct choice or a full model answer.

Like the A-level app, show **Hint** after a ten-second delay, and **More help** after another ten seconds. The independent "I don't know — show me" action remains available immediately. Correct first *physical* taps on ordinary four-choice questions may auto-check; an initial wrong tap shows no correction, and pupils then press Check. Keyboard selection should not auto-check.

## 6. Diagrams as questions and feedback

- Prefer **original, accurate, legible SVGs** for human tissues, cell components, endocrine loops, nerve pathways, inheritance, growth curves, reproductive anatomy, blood films and trial structures.
- **Question mode:** clean, minimally labelled image; prominent A/B marker; four plausible choices. The accessible image description should explain the layout *without announcing the answer*.
- **Feedback mode:** show the same figure, reveal the relevant name or causal relationship, and add no more than 1–2 explanatory sentences.
- Use figures in other formats when they help: e.g., a simple graph to interpret before calculating, or a sequence of arrows for process ordering.
- Check that labels, lines and visual symbols remain distinguishable at phone size and for students with limited colour perception.
- Do not substitute a diagram for a question that is better answered with a single simple sentence.

## 7. Maths and practical reasoning

- Add a **calculator cue** on calculations, and where needed a concise "Write down the equation" prompt before applying it.
- Prefer sensible accessible values first, then decimals, units and linked reasoning. For example: 20 ÷ 5 before a complex magnification conversion.
- Visual feedback can mark the relevant axes or calculation relationship, then show units and each conversion.
- Use examples of experimental validity, repeatability, dilution, controls, chromatography, microscopy or clinical measurements as **generic disciplinary knowledge**. No template or step-by-step support for a live NEA task.
- Distinguish "correct answer in the expected unit" from "correct number with wrong units"; test the input parser rather than silently crediting malformed strings.

## 8. Coursework-unit boundary: general knowledge, not assignment support

OCR F170/F171 are examined. Units F172–F177 are NEA-assessed, with restrictions on set assignments and on feedback during/after work. The app **may** support learning underlying genetics, laboratory methods, nutrition, reproduction, neuroscience and pharmacology. It **must not**:
- reproduce the OCR-set assignments, invent lookalike substitute assignments or create practice tasks mimicking an assessed assignment;
- use a real assignment scenario or case details to suggest evidence, analysis, wording, layout, method, improvements, grade or a planned response;
- accept, annotate, mark or grade student NEA work, or help revise an assessed submission;
- create personalised plans, patient reports, clinical recommendations, research proposals or investigation outputs in the form students are required to submit for their coursework.

The app's **Other units** pathway is therefore labelled "Knowledge practice only". Students can use it for subject fluency *before* assessment and for general learning alongside teaching; schools must still enforce current supervised-session rules and their own assessment policies.

The app's practical support should look like **"What does chromatography separate?"**, **"Why use a control?"**, **"Which part of a uterus is labelled A?"**, or **"What does random allocation reduce?"**, not a clinical investigation template or guidance on how to earn M/D criteria.

## 9. Review gates

For every question:
1. Confirm the OCR unit and broad teaching-content reference, and **mark incomplete mapping honestly**.
2. Check exactly one intended answer (or a clear, constrained self-check rubric) and review distractors for misconceptions.
3. Check language load, student accessibility, unusual vocabulary, maths difficulty and any diagram's scientific accuracy.
4. Verify that hint steps never contain the answer and can be used without artificially inflating independent scores.
5. Test answer formatting, quick-tap rules, cooldowns, correct/wrong/unknown paths and local-only progress.
6. Verify visually on desktop and mobile; automated syntax and assertion tests are **not a substitute for teacher scientific review or visual testing**.
7. Check that no question or feedback in F172–F177 is tied to assessed assignment evidence or model coursework.
